01
THE SETUP

Three clocks, three speeds

The last commodity supercycle was built on China’s WTO integration: factories, ports, housing, steel. The next one, if it comes, is being built on AI infrastructure: power, data centers, copper. The common thread is not “demand” — it is demand growing faster than supply can respond, for long enough to matter.

1.1 The last supercycle: demand outran supply

China’s 2001 WTO entry did not create a sudden comparative advantage; it reduced the uncertainty around trade rules, which made global companies more willing to build factories and supply chains in China. Manufacturing reallocated, emerging consumers got cheaper goods, and China had to build factories, ports, roads, grids, and housing. Those activities require steel, copper, aluminum, and energy.

Demand rose within a few years. Supply could not. Mines and energy projects take years to permit and build. When prices were high, miners invested; when those mines came online around 2011, China’s property and infrastructure demand was slowing. The result was a classic cobweb cycle: investment at the top, supply at the bottom. S&P Global data show the global nonferrous exploration budget fell from ~$21.5B in 2012 as the industry retrenched.

1.2 AI: capex first, productivity later

AI is not WTO. WTO reallocated labor and manufacturing; AI automates information processing and changes workflows. But the timing has a familiar shape: the capex arrives before the productivity. Before AI shows up in aggregate growth, companies must build servers, data centers, power generation, and transmission. The IEA expects global data-center electricity use to rise from ~415 TWh in 2024 to ~945 TWh in 2030.

That capex is financeable. Data centers produce recurring cash flow — rent, cloud services, long-term contracts — and that cash flow can support asset values and debt. The mechanism is closer to infrastructure than to a dot-com story, provided the cash flow is real. That “provided” is the whole report.

Key judgment

AI demand is narrower than China’s WTO/property boom. It pulls power, copper, cooling, chips, and grid equipment — not steel, cement, appliances, local services, and broad consumer demand. This is a narrow supercycle, not a repeat of 2001–2011.

1.3 Three clocks, three speeds

Productivity runs in years. Credit runs in quarters. Supply runs in decades. Financial markets can reprice risk in weeks; AI adoption may take years; a large mine can take 10–17 years. A supercycle requires the three to overlap.

1.4 Supply: the slowest clock

Copper is the cleanest example. The IEA estimates that average copper mine grade has fallen ~40% since 1991, and a new mine takes ~17 years from discovery to production. S&P Global data show 2026 copper exploration budgets up 32% to ~$4.3B — the highest since 2012. The industry is responding to price, but exploration is not production; the lag is the point.

One nuance: low copper TC/RCs reflect tightness between mined concentrate and smelter capacity. That is not the same as an equally tight refined-copper market. The real test is inventories, TC/RCs, and the mine ramp-up curve — not a single price or spread.

Do not confuse “long-term right” with “short-term safe”

If AI financing contracts, leverage unwinds, or real demand weakens, commodities can fall sharply even with long-term supply constraints. Long-term supply does not protect against a short-term credit hit.

02
THE PHYSICAL BOTTLENECK

The US is short of electrons and copper

The US is not running out of ideas. It is running out of electrons, copper, and patience with permitting. AI capex is colliding with a grid that was built for a flatter demand world — and with a copper supply chain that takes decades to respond.

2.1 Power: the binding constraint

US data centers consumed about 176 TWh in 2023, or 4.4% of US electricity. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) projects 325–580 TWh by 2028, or 6.7–12% of US power. That is a 2–3x increase in five years, in a system where load growth had been roughly flat for two decades.

The price signal is already visible. The BLS average US retail electricity price (FRED: APU000072610) rose from 13.5¢/kWh in 2020 to 19.6¢/kWh in September 2026 — a ~45% increase. The bottleneck is not just generation: LBNL estimates about 2,600 GW of generation and storage sits in US interconnection queues, roughly twice the installed US fleet. Turbines, transformers, transmission, and permits are the slow variables.

Natural gas is not the constraint. EIA storage (series NW2_EPG0_SWO_R48_BCF) is 3,644 Bcf, in the 89th percentile of the last five years. Henry Hub (FRED: DHHNGSP) averaged about $3.47/MMBtu in 2026. The molecules are there; the infrastructure to turn them into delivered power is not.

2.2 Energy: tight in liquids, loose in gas

The EIA weekly data tell a split story. Commercial crude inventories are 412.5 million barrels, the 5th percentile of the last five years (series WCESTUS1). Distillate is 104.3 million (7th, WDISTUS1); gasoline is 205.3 million (1st, WGTSTUS1); the Strategic Petroleum Reserve is 282.7 million (0th, WCSSTUS1). Cushing is the exception at 27.6 million (64th, W_EPC0_SAX_YCUOK_MBBL).

US crude production is 13.27 million b/d (WCRFPUS2), exports are 3.44 million b/d (WCREXUS2), and refinery utilization is 93.4% (WPULEUS3). The physical market is tight in the products that matter for transportation and industry — but not uniformly tight. The EIA STEO (October 2026) has Brent at $111 in October 2026, $90 by April 2027, and $72 by December 2027, a reminder that “tight” is not the same as “$150.”

2.3 Copper: the supply chain cannot hurry

Copper is the purest “narrow supercycle” metal. The IEA estimates average mine grade has fallen ~40% since 1991; a new mine takes ~17 years from discovery to production. S&P Global data show 2026 copper exploration budgets up 32% to ~$4.3B, the highest since 2012 — but exploration is not production.

FRED’s monthly global copper price (PCOPPUSDM) rose from ~$12,987/t in January 2026 to $14,473/t in September 2026 (+11.4%). The US is structurally short: it imports roughly half of its copper (USGS), and a new US mine can take a decade or more to permit. Low TC/RCs reflect concentrate tightness, not necessarily an equally tight refined market — watch inventories and the mine ramp-up curve.

The US bottleneck in one line

Power is the new land. The US has gas, capital, and demand. What it lacks is delivered electricity, copper, transformers, transmission, and permits. That is why the AI trade is a physical trade — and why it cannot be measured by chip shipments alone.

03
THE US CREDIT LAYER

America is trying to collateralize compute

The US is not replacing the dollar. It is trying to collateralize the next credit cycle with power and compute. The fiscal backdrop makes that urgent; the data-center financing market makes it possible; the credit market is already voting on whether it is real.

3.1 The fiscal backdrop: debt needs a new story

Federal debt was about $39.5 trillion in 2026Q2 (FRED: GFDEBTN). Federal interest outlays were about $970 billion in FY2025 (FRED: FYOINT), and the CBO projects publicly held debt/GDP rising from 101% in FY2026 to 120% in FY2036, with the deficit widening from 5.8% to 6.7% of GDP. The US needs persistent foreign capital. The old story was “Treasuries + oil.” The new story is “Treasuries + energy + compute.”

The rates market is the scorekeeper. The 10-year yield is 4.96% (FRED: DGS10), the 30-year is 5.29% (DGS30), the 5-year is 4.78% (DGS5), and the 2s10s curve is +31bp (T10Y2Y). The 10-year real yield is 2.64% (DFII10) and the 10-year breakeven is 2.34% (T10YIE). Real yields at 2.6% are a high bar for any long-duration asset — including data centers.

3.2 Data-center finance: the new credit asset

Data centers are becoming financeable assets. Data-center project finance is $106 billion year-to-date, versus ~$37 billion in all of 2025. AI-related credit issuance is ~$466 billion YTD. S&P Global counts 32 rated North American data-center infrastructure deals with ~$105 billion of related debt. This is not yet a 2007-style housing market, but the mechanism is familiar: recurring cash flow supports asset value, asset value supports financing, and financing builds more capacity.

The credit market is also starting to differentiate. AI HY spreads are about 360bp; the broad HY index is 340bp (FRED: BAMLH0A0HYM2); CCC is 1,340bp (BAMLH0A3HYC); IG is 89bp (BAMLC0A0CM). Data-center HY bonds have fallen from above 101 in June to ~95. The index looks calm; the tail is cracking.

3.3 The collateral re-anchor

The dollar’s real anchor is still the Treasury market. What is changing is the collateral behind the next credit cycle: power, data centers, and the cash flows they produce. If those cash flows are real, the US has a new, investable asset class that can absorb capital and support the dollar’s asset appeal. If they are not, the credit market will reprice the collateral — and the capex will stop.

The dollar’s reserve share is a slow variable, not a cliff. IMF COFER data show the USD share falling from 71.2% in 1999Q1 to 56.7% in 2026Q2; USD reserve holdings are $7.495 trillion, below the 2021Q3 peak of $7.677 trillion, while total reserves are $13.217 trillion. This is a migration, not a collapse — and it is not the main driver of the next 12 months.

The US risk in one line

Compute is an asset, not an anchor. If AI returns disappoint, data-center cash flow will not cover the capex, credit spreads will widen before equity prices reflect it, and the “energy + compute” collateral story will fade. The 2027–2028 refinancing wall is the timer, not 2026.

04
THE CONTRAST

China: a contrast, not the main story

China is running a state-led version of the same physical buildout. It is a real source of copper, power-equipment, and grid demand. It is smaller, narrower, and not a 1:1 replacement for its property boom — but the credit-expansion mechanism rhymes. For US investors, China is a demand contrast, not the driver of the US credit story.

Visual summary · not a data chart

4.1 The old engine is fading

China’s property sector was the collateral engine of its credit system: household mortgages, land and property pledges, and local-government land revenue reinforced each other. That engine is now contracting. Real-estate development investment fell from ~14.8 trillion RMB in 2021 to ~10.0 trillion RMB in 2024 (−32%); local land-sale revenue fell from ~8.7 trillion to ~4.9 trillion RMB (−44%). The credit impulse that replaced it will not be the same size or shape.

4.2 The new engine is state-led compute

Beijing’s response is a state-led buildout of computing infrastructure: 8 national computing hubs and 10 data-center clusters, with 400+ billion RMB of planned investment. Data-center electricity is projected to rise from ~170 TWh in 2025 to 800 TWh in 2030; the 15th Five-Year Plan calls for 5+ trillion RMB of grid investment. This is real physical demand — but it is policy-driven, bank-financed, and more prone to “build first, find demand later.”

4.3 Why it matters to US readers

  • It adds a demand floor, not a new price regime. Chinese grid, copper, and power-equipment demand can tighten global markets, but it does not change the US credit cycle or the dollar’s anchor.
  • It is narrower than property. Property pulled steel, cement, appliances, and local services; compute pulls power, copper, cooling, and chips. The commodity impact is concentrated, not broad.
  • It is smaller. China’s “East Data West Compute” planned investment is ~3% of its 2021 property investment peak. Even if private capex doubles the total, it remains a single-digit percentage of the property boom.
  • It is a different pricing mechanism. The US prices data-center collateral in credit spreads and ABS; China prices it through state banks and policy. That is why the same physical theme can have very different risk profiles.
The nuance: same mechanism, different asset

Computing power is not the new real estate — but the credit-expansion mechanism is analogous. A data center can be pledged as collateral, financed with debt, and used by local governments or SOEs to create investment, just as property once was. The differences are scale (~3% of the property peak), demand breadth (power and copper, not steel, cement, and appliances), and asset life (3–7 years vs 20–30 years). Not the same asset; the same credit logic.

Bottom line on China

China is a demand contrast, not the main story. It can tighten copper and power-equipment markets, but it cannot fill the US credit gap or replace the dollar’s Treasury anchor. For a US audience, the investable signal is in US power, US data-center cash flow, and US credit spreads — with China as a secondary physical-demand cross-check.

05
SCENARIOS

Three scenarios for US investors

These are conditional scenarios, not forecasts. The difference is not whether AI is “good.” It is whether AI’s returns can become durable cash flow and credit expansion — and whether the physical supply chain can respond before the credit cycle turns.

5.1 Scenario 1: AI works — cash flow before the credit window closes

AI drives real productivity gains, enterprise revenue rises, data centers generate stable cash flow, and project finance remains open. Copper, aluminum, power, and grid equipment enter a multi-year demand cycle. Gold lags as real yields and risk appetite normalize; credit spreads tighten. Watch: data-center occupancy, PPA signings, actual power demand, ABS/project-finance spreads, and AI HY falling below 250bp.

5.2 Scenario 2: AI disappoints — the credit cycle turns first

AI capex is cut, projects are delayed, and data-center cash flow does not cover the cost of capital. Credit spreads widen, AI HY and CCC lead, and the refinancing window closes. Gold and high-quality duration benefit as real yields fall; industrial metals lag because real demand weakens. Watch: AI capex guidance, project delays, AI HY above 450bp, CCC above 1,500bp, and data-center HY bonds below 90.

5.3 Scenario 3: AI is real, but not enough — the base case

AI is real but insufficient to solve the debt problem. Growth and debt adjustment coexist. Copper is supported by grid and infrastructure spending plus supply constraints; gold is supported by real rates, credit risk, and reserve demand; silver gets both, with higher volatility. This is the scenario we spend most time on — the “narrow supercycle.” Watch: copper TC/RCs, inventories, mine ramp-ups, COFER, central-bank gold reserves, and real yields.

5.4 Near-term risk: survive the credit contraction first

Long-term supply constraints do not protect against a short-term credit hit. If AI financing contracts or leverage unwinds, commodities can fall sharply even as the supply story remains intact. The credit market is already flashing: AI HY 360bp, CCC 1,340bp, data-center HY bonds from 101 to ~95, and a 2027–2028 refinancing wall.

That is why the card is 30% invested and 58% cash. It is not a bearish call on copper or gold. It is a recognition that the credit cycle can turn before the physical cycle does. Survive the credit contraction first; then talk about the supercycle.

The time-scale mismatch

Markets can reprice risk in weeks; AI adoption takes years; a large mine takes a decade or more. When the clocks overlap, you get a supercycle. When they do not, you get a price drawdown and a broken narrative. Do not use long-term supply as a substitute for a stop-loss.

6.3 What would change our mind

Upgrade the supercycle case

  • Data-center occupancy, revenue, and PPAs beat expectations; actual power demand accelerates.
  • Data-center ABS/project-finance spreads tighten; AI HY falls below 250bp.
  • Copper TC/RCs stay low while inventories do not build; mine ramp-ups delay.
  • COFER USD share keeps falling; central banks keep buying gold.

Downgrade the supercycle case

  • AI capex is cut; data-center projects are delayed; utilization falls.
  • AI HY >450bp, CCC >1,500bp, data-center HY bonds below 90.
  • Copper inventories build sharply; TC/RCs recover; mine supply accelerates.
  • The dollar stabilizes/strengthens; real yields rise; gold-reserve demand fades.

6.4 Conclusion

The next commodity supercycle is not a simple “AI demand” story, and not a “dollar collapse, gold to the moon” story. It is a collateral re-anchor: the US is trying to collateralize the next credit cycle with power and compute. That is where the investable signals are.

We hold two judgments: long-term conditions are forming; the short-term regime is not confirmed. Copper’s supply constraints and power’s physical bottleneck are real, but AI financing contraction and wider credit spreads can arrive first. The trade can be right; the regime is not confirmed.

“The next supercycle won’t be announced by a demand forecast. It will show up in a data-center cash flow, a copper TC/RC, and a credit spread.”

A.4 Methodology and limitations

This is a scenario analysis, not a forecast. Scenario probabilities are not quantified because the key variables — AI productivity, data-center cash flow, policy response — lack a sufficient historical sample. “Base case” means where we spend the most research time, not a probability. China’s data have definitional differences (compute scale, racks, electricity, investment); we prioritize cross-checkable electricity and planned-investment data. US data-center finance figures come from a credit review and may differ from public-market statistics. All data are as of 2026-10-25 unless otherwise noted.
